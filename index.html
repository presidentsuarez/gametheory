Chapter One
Starting From Zero
WHAT IS
GAME THEORY?
Before we dive into math, let's get the core idea completely clear — no jargon, just logic.

The Big Idea

Imagine you and your friend both want the last slice of pizza. What you do depends on what your friend does. And what your friend does depends on what you do. That's it. That's game theory.

Game theory is the scientific study of strategic interaction — situations where the outcome for you depends not just on your own choices, but on the choices of others.

It doesn't matter if we're talking about two countries deciding whether to go to war, two companies setting prices, or two siblings negotiating who does the dishes. The same mathematical logic applies.

The Formal Definition

A game in game theory has three ingredients:


1. Players — who is making decisions?

2. Strategies — what choices does each player have?

3. Payoffs — what does each player get for each combination of choices?


That's the complete recipe. Everything else is just working out the math.

Why Does This Matter?

Before game theory, economists assumed people made decisions in isolation — just "maximize your own outcome." But that's almost never how the world works. Almost every important decision you make involves other people reacting to you.

01 — BUSINESS
Pricing Wars
When Walmart lowers prices, Target has to decide whether to match them. When Target reacts, Walmart reacts to that. This infinite loop is a game.
02 — POLITICS
Nuclear Deterrence
The Cold War was kept "cold" because both superpowers understood the game: mutual destruction means neither side should shoot first.
03 — DAILY LIFE
Traffic Jams
Every driver choosing the "fastest" route creates congestion that makes everyone slower. Game theory predicted this — and explains why GPS apps sometimes make it worse.

A Brief History

1928
John von Neumann
Proved the minimax theorem — that in any two-player zero-sum game, there's always an optimal strategy. Game theory is born.
1944
Theory of Games and Economic Behavior
Von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern publish the bible of game theory. Economics would never be the same.
1950
John Nash — The Equilibrium
Nash, a 21-year-old PhD student at Princeton, proves his famous equilibrium theorem in a 27-page dissertation. He wins the Nobel Prize 44 years later.
1994
Nobel Prize in Economics
Nash, Harsanyi, and Selten win the Nobel for their contributions to game theory — cementing it as one of the most important ideas in social science.
Today
Everywhere
Used in AI, biology (evolution), auction design, climate negotiations, antitrust law, and social media algorithms. It's the language of strategic thinking.

Chapter Two
The Most Famous Game Ever
THE PRISONER'S
DILEMMA
A story about two criminals that reveals the darkest truth in all of social science.

The Story

Two suspects — let's call them Alex and Blake — are arrested for a crime. The police separate them immediately so they can't communicate. Each is offered the same deal:

THE DEAL ON THE TABLE

🔇 If you stay silent (Cooperate) and your partner also stays silent: both get 1 year in prison. Minor charge, not much evidence.

🗣️ If you betray (Defect) your partner and they stay silent: you go free, they get 5 years.

🗣️🗣️ If you both betray each other: you both get 3 years. Reduced sentence for cooperating with police, but you're both convicted.

You have 30 seconds. You can't talk to each other. What do you do?

Payoff Matrix — Years in Prison (Lower = Better)
Blake: Silent (Cooperate) Blake: Betray (Defect)
Alex: Silent (Cooperate) Alex: 1yr, Blake: 1yr ✓ Alex: 5yr, Blake: 0yr
Alex: Betray (Defect) Alex: 0yr, Blake: 5yr Alex: 3yr, Blake: 3yr ★
Best collective outcome (Pareto Optimal) Nash Equilibrium (what rational players actually do)

The Brutal Logic

Here's why rational Alex always betrays — no matter what:

Alex's Thinking — If Blake stays silent

"If Blake stays silent, I get 1 year if I'm silent... but 0 years if I betray. Better to betray."

Alex's Thinking — If Blake betrays

"If Blake betrays, I get 5 years if I'm silent... but only 3 years if I betray back. Better to betray."

Betray is the right move whether Blake is silent or betraying. It's what game theorists call a dominant strategy — it's always better regardless of what the other player does.

Blake reasons the exact same way. So both betray. Both get 3 years.

But wait — if they'd both stayed silent, they'd only get 1 year each. They just made each other worse off by being rational.

The Paradox

The Core Tragedy

Individual rationality leads to collective irrationality. Each person doing what's best for themselves creates an outcome that's worse for everyone.


This is why we have:

• Cartels that fall apart (everyone cheats)

• Arms races (both countries build weapons, both less safe)

• Overfishing (rational fishermen destroy the fishery)

• Doping in sports (everyone dopes, no one gains advantage)

• Climate change (each country pollutes, all suffer)


The Prisoner's Dilemma isn't just a thought experiment. It's the structure of most of the world's hardest problems.

But What If You Play Multiple Rounds?

Here's where it gets really interesting. The one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma says: always defect. But in real life, we interact with the same people over and over. This changes everything.

Tit for Tat — The Legendary Strategy

In 1980, political scientist Robert Axelrod ran a tournament: submit a strategy for iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and see which beats all others over 200 rounds.

The winner, submitted by Anatol Rapoport, was shockingly simple:

The Tit for Tat Rules

Round 1: Cooperate.

Every round after: Do exactly what your opponent did last round. If they cooperated, cooperate. If they defected, defect.

That's it. Four lines. Yet it beat every complex strategy submitted. The lesson: be nice, be retaliatory, be forgiving, be clear. These four properties — encoded in the simplest possible way — turn out to be the foundation of cooperation in nature and society.

Always Defect — The Cynic's Strategy

The "obvious" rational strategy: never cooperate, no matter what. Exploit every cooperative player you meet.

Why It Fails Long-Term

Always Defect wins against naive cooperators but fails catastrophically against Tit for Tat and other retaliatory strategies. In Axelrod's tournament, it finished near the bottom. Bad reputation compounds.

This is why genuinely exploitative strategies tend to destroy themselves — they poison the environment they rely on. Once everyone knows you always defect, nobody cooperates with you.

Always Cooperate — The Naive Idealist

Cooperate every single round, no matter what your opponent does. Pure altruism.

Why It Fails

Gets absolutely massacred by Always Defect. An always-cooperative player is an infinite resource for exploiters. Finishing near the bottom in most tournaments, it demonstrates that unconditional niceness — without any retaliation mechanism — is not a stable strategy.

The insight: cooperation requires the credible threat of retaliation. Pure kindness without any teeth isn't a strategy — it's an invitation.

Grim Trigger — The Nuclear Option

Cooperate until your opponent defects even once. Then defect forever, for the rest of the game. No forgiveness.

The Psychology

Extremely effective at preventing defection because the punishment is so severe. But one mistake — accidental or misinterpreted — locks both players into permanent mutual defection. It's the game-theoretic equivalent of MAD (Mutually Assured Destruction).

Grim Trigger performs well mathematically but poorly in noisy environments where miscommunication happens. The real world is noisy. This is why Tit for Tat — which forgives after one retaliatory defection — tends to outperform it.

Chapter Three
The Central Concept
NASH
EQUILIBRIUM
The idea that won John Nash the Nobel Prize — and changed how we understand everything from traffic to war.

What Is It, Really?

A Nash Equilibrium is a situation where nobody can do better by changing only their own strategy, given what everyone else is doing.

In other words: it's a stable state. Everyone has made their best possible response to everyone else. No one has any incentive to change.

It doesn't mean everyone is happy. It doesn't mean the outcome is good. It just means the situation is stable — no one player can unilaterally improve their position.

The Classic Analogy

Imagine everyone in a concert hall choosing where to sit. A Nash Equilibrium is reached when nobody would prefer to move to a different empty seat, given where everyone else is sitting.


Notice: this might leave many good seats empty. The outcome can be inefficient. But it's stable — no individual has reason to change.

Example: Where to Open Your Restaurant?

Suppose two ice cream vendors, Mia and Leo, are setting up on a beach. Beachgoers are spread evenly from one end to the other. Where should each vendor go?

The Ice Cream Vendor Game

Mia's logic: "If Leo sets up at the quarter-mile mark, I should set up just next to him — toward the center — to capture more than half the beach."

Leo's logic: "If Mia moved toward center to steal my customers, I should also move toward center."

The result: Both vendors end up right in the middle of the beach. Nobody can gain by moving. That's the Nash Equilibrium.

The cost: Beachgoers at both ends now have to walk much further. The socially optimal solution — vendors at the quarter marks — is not the equilibrium. Individual rationality again produces collective inefficiency.

This explains why gas stations cluster together, why political parties converge to the center, and why TV networks all show similar content in primetime.

Nash's Key Insight: Equilibria Always Exist

What Nash proved — and this is the mathematical genius — is that every finite game has at least one Nash Equilibrium, if you allow for "mixed strategies" (randomizing your choices).

Mixed Strategies — What Does That Mean?

Sometimes your best strategy is to randomize. In Rock-Paper-Scissors, if you always play Rock, your opponent will always play Paper. The only Nash Equilibrium is to play each option with exactly 1/3 probability — then your opponent can't exploit you.


This is why poker players sometimes bluff with bad hands. If you never bluff, opponents can read you perfectly. Randomization is strategically essential.

Multiple Equilibria: The Coordination Problem

Some games have multiple Nash Equilibria — and figuring out which one will happen is one of game theory's deepest puzzles.

Battle of the Sexes — Two Equilibria Exist
Sam: OperaSam: Football
Jordan: Opera Jordan: 2, Sam: 1 ★ Jordan: 0, Sam: 0
Jordan: Football Jordan: 0, Sam: 0 Jordan: 1, Sam: 2 ★

Jordan prefers Opera. Sam prefers Football. But both prefer being together over being apart. There are two Nash Equilibria — both at Opera, or both at Football.

Which one happens? The math doesn't say. This is where focal points (Thomas Schelling's insight) come in — when there's no communication, people gravitate to obvious, culturally salient options.

Schelling's Focal Point Test

"If you had to meet a stranger in New York City tomorrow, with no prior communication about where or when — where would you go?"


Most people say: noon, under the clock at Grand Central Station. Nobody coordinated. Nobody agreed. The answer just feels obvious. That's a focal point.

Chapter Four
The Taxonomy
TYPES OF
GAMES
Not all strategic situations are the same. Here's how to classify them — and why the classification matters.
Dimension 01
Zero-Sum vs Non-Zero-Sum

Zero-sum: One player's gain is exactly another's loss. Total winnings always equal zero. Chess, poker, war. Pure conflict.


Non-zero-sum: Players can all gain, or all lose, simultaneously. Most economic interactions, negotiations, climate deals. Cooperation can create value.


Why it matters: Zero-sum thinking applied to non-zero-sum games destroys value. Many business "negotiations" are actually non-zero-sum — there are trades that benefit both sides, but people miss them by treating it as combat.

Dimension 02
Simultaneous vs Sequential

Simultaneous: Players choose at the same time (or without knowing others' choices). Analyzed with Nash Equilibrium. Rock-Paper-Scissors, sealed bids.


Sequential: Players move in turns, seeing what came before. Analyzed with Backward Induction. Chess, negotiation with offers and counteroffers.


Why it matters: Moving first can be an advantage (chess openings) or a disadvantage (showing your hand). The structure of timing completely changes the strategic logic.

Dimension 03
Perfect vs Imperfect Information

Perfect information: Every player knows the complete history of the game. Chess. Everything visible.


Imperfect information: Some actions are hidden. Poker. You don't know opponents' cards.


Incomplete information: Players don't even know others' payoffs or "types." You don't know if the other negotiator is desperate or has options.


Why it matters: Imperfect information is where bluffing, signaling, and screening strategies come alive.

Dimension 04
Cooperative vs Non-Cooperative

Non-cooperative: Players act individually. Most of classical game theory. Nash Equilibrium is the solution concept.


Cooperative: Players can form binding coalitions and negotiate collectively. How should the gains from cooperation be split? Analyzed with the Shapley Value.


Example: Three companies merge. How do you fairly divide profits given each contributed differently? The Shapley Value — used by everything from cost accounting to AI interpretability — answers this.

The Stag Hunt — Trust and Risk

Two hunters can jointly hunt a stag (big prize, requires both) or each hunt a hare alone (small prize, guaranteed). This game reveals the tension between safety and social cooperation.

Stag Hunt Payoff Matrix
Hunter B: Hunt StagHunter B: Hunt Hare
Hunter A: Hunt Stag A: 4, B: 4 — Best! ✓ A: 0, B: 2
Hunter A: Hunt Hare A: 2, B: 0 A: 2, B: 2 ★

Unlike the Prisoner's Dilemma, here both Nash Equilibria are pure cooperation or pure safety. The best outcome (4,4) is achievable — but only if both players trust each other.

The hare hunt (2,2) is the risk-dominant equilibrium — safer to guarantee yourself 2 than risk getting 0 if the other defects.

The stag hunt (4,4) is the payoff-dominant equilibrium — better for everyone, but requires trust.

Real World: Climate Agreements

Countries reducing carbon emissions is a stag hunt. If everyone cooperates, everyone benefits enormously. But if one country doesn't cooperate, cooperating countries sacrifice their economies for nothing.


The game theory solution: binding agreements, verification mechanisms, and reputation effects — turning the one-shot stag hunt into a repeated game where cooperation becomes stable.

Chapter Five
It's Everywhere
GAME THEORY
IN THE WILD
This isn't academic theory. It's the operating system running underneath every negotiation, market, and conflict on earth.
🎰
Spectrum Auctions

When the US government sells radio spectrum to telecom companies, they use auction formats designed entirely by game theorists. The FCC's 1994 auction raised $617 million — far more than expected — because the auction design accounted for bidders' strategic behavior. The 2020 C-Band auction raised $81 billion. Game theory literally designed the mechanism.

🤖
AI and Machine Learning

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) — the AI behind deepfakes and AI art — are literally two-player zero-sum games. A generator tries to fool a discriminator; the discriminator tries to catch the generator. They reach Nash Equilibrium when the generator makes perfect fakes. The entire architecture is applied game theory.

Reinforcement learning, used to train AlphaGo, Chess AI, and modern language models, is solving massive sequential games with imperfect information.

🦠
Evolutionary Biology

Evolution plays game theory without consciousness. Hawks (aggressive) vs Doves (peaceful) competing for resources reaches a Nash Equilibrium mixture of both strategies in a population. Too many hawks, and hawks start killing each other — doves gain. Too many doves, and hawks dominate. The equilibrium proportion is mathematically derivable.

This is why animal populations often stabilize at predictable sex ratios, territorial sizes, and aggression levels — they're at evolutionary Nash Equilibria.

🏦
Financial Markets

Stock markets are multi-player games with imperfect information. Flash crashes occur when algorithmic traders all reach the same Nash Equilibrium simultaneously — pull out — creating a cascade. The 2010 Flash Crash erased $1 trillion in market value in minutes because trading algorithms all made the same "rational" move.

Market makers, arbitrageurs, and high-frequency traders are constantly playing iterated games against each other. The bid-ask spread is the equilibrium outcome of that competition.

🏥
Matching Markets — The Kidney Exchange

Nobel Laureate Al Roth applied game theory to design kidney exchange programs. The problem: Patient A needs a kidney, and their donor isn't compatible. Patient B has the same problem. But A's donor is compatible with B, and B's donor is compatible with A. Roth designed matching algorithms — solving a complex cooperative game — that have saved thousands of lives. He also redesigned how US medical students are matched to hospitals, fixing a broken market using mechanism design.

⚛️
Nuclear Deterrence

MAD — Mutually Assured Destruction — is a Nash Equilibrium. If Country A fires first, Country B retaliates and both are destroyed. If neither fires, both survive. Neither country can improve by unilaterally changing its strategy (assuming the retaliation threat is credible). The entire Cold War was game theory playing out at geopolitical scale.

Thomas Schelling won the Nobel Prize for showing how commitment devices — burning bridges, making retaliation automatic — make deterrence credible. His work directly influenced nuclear policy.

Chapter Six — Advanced
For the Serious Student
ADVANCED
CONCEPTS
You understand the foundation. Now let's go deeper — the ideas that power modern economics, AI, and political science.

Signaling Theory

How do you communicate private information credibly? If you know something the other party doesn't, how do they know you're not lying?

Why Do People Get MBAs?

Michael Spence (Nobel Prize, 2001) asked: Does an MBA actually make you more productive — or does it just signal that you were already smart and hardworking enough to complete it?

His model: employers can't directly observe your ability. But getting an MBA is painful and expensive. If low-ability workers find it too costly to fake the signal, only high-ability workers will get MBAs. Employers rationally reward MBA holders — not because of the skills taught, but because the degree credibly separates the able from the less able.

The signal works precisely because it's costly. Free signals are ignored. This is why peacocks have absurd tails, why luxury goods have visible logos, and why hazing exists in fraternities.

Mechanism Design — "Reverse Game Theory"

Normal game theory: given a game, find the equilibrium. Mechanism design: given the outcome you want, design the game that produces it.

The Designer's Problem

You're running an auction. Bidders know their own valuations but not others'. You want to maximize revenue and efficiency. What auction format do you use?


William Vickrey proved (Nobel Prize, 1996): In a second-price auction (you win by bidding highest, but pay the second-highest bid), it's always in your interest to bid your true valuation. The mechanism extracts honest behavior from self-interested players.


This insight powers Google's ad auctions, eBay, and most modern online marketplaces. Every time you use Google, mechanism design is running silently underneath.

Revenue Equivalence Theorem: Under certain conditions, all standard auction formats (English, Dutch, first-price, second-price) yield the same expected revenue.

Bayesian Games — Playing with Uncertainty

Real-world games rarely have complete information. You don't know your opponent's costs, their desperation, their outside options. Harsanyi (Nobel Prize, 1994) formalized how to handle this.

The Poker Example

You're holding a weak hand. Should you bluff? It depends on what you think your opponent believes about your hand — and what they think you think they believe...


Harsanyi's insight: model each player as a random "type" drawn from a distribution. Rational players form beliefs (priors) about others' types and update using Bayes' theorem as the game unfolds. The equilibrium concept is Bayesian Nash Equilibrium.

Application: Negotiations

In salary negotiations, you don't know the company's budget. They don't know your other offers. Both sides signal (sometimes honestly, sometimes not).


Game theory predicts: screening mechanisms emerge — companies ask for salary history, put out wide ranges, use take-it-or-leave-it offers — all designed to extract private information without giving it away.

Subgame Perfection — Credible Threats

Nash Equilibrium has a flaw: it can include equilibria sustained by threats that would never actually be carried out.

The Entry Deterrence Game

Setup: You're a new company thinking of entering a market dominated by Megacorp. Megacorp threatens: "If you enter, we'll start a price war and destroy both of us."

The Nash Equilibrium says: If you believe the threat, don't enter. Megacorp never has to follow through. Threat works.

But is it credible? If you actually enter, does Megacorp want to start a price war? No — the price war hurts Megacorp too. The threat is empty. You should call the bluff.

Subgame Perfect Equilibrium (Reinhard Selten, Nobel 1994) eliminates equilibria sustained by non-credible threats using backward induction. It predicts: you enter, Megacorp accommodates.

Backward Induction — The Key Technique

To find what rational players do in a sequential game: start at the end. What would a rational player do in the last move? Then work backwards. What would they do in the second-to-last move, knowing what comes after? Continue until you reach the first move.


Chess computers use this (to enormous depth). CEOs use this in merger negotiations. Lawyers use this in settlement decisions. It's the logic of thinking ahead.

The Shapley Value — Fair Division

Three lawyers form a firm. Lawyer A alone earns $100k. Lawyer B alone earns $150k. Lawyers A+B together earn $400k (synergy!). Add Lawyer C and together they earn $600k. How do you fairly split the $600k?

Lloyd Shapley's Answer (Nobel Prize, 2012)

Calculate each player's average marginal contribution across all possible orderings in which they could join the coalition. That's their fair share.


The Shapley Value is the unique solution satisfying four axioms: efficiency, symmetry, dummy player, and additivity. It's used in cost allocation, profit splitting, voting power analysis, and — strikingly — explaining neural networks (SHAP values in ML explainability).

Chapter Seven
The Hard Truths
WHERE GAME
THEORY FAILS
No framework is complete. Here's where classical game theory breaks down — and what comes next.
LIMIT 01
The Rationality Assumption
Classical game theory assumes players are perfectly rational and self-interested. But humans are systematically irrational. In the Ultimatum Game, players routinely reject "unfair" offers even when accepting any amount beats getting nothing — sacrificing money to punish perceived injustice. Emotions, norms, and fairness drive behavior in ways pure rationality can't predict.
LIMIT 02
Equilibrium Selection
When multiple Nash Equilibria exist, game theory often can't say which one players will reach. Refinements (subgame perfection, trembling hand perfection) help but don't fully solve the problem. In practice, history, culture, communication, and focal points determine outcomes in ways the math struggles to capture.
LIMIT 03
Computational Complexity
Finding Nash Equilibria is computationally hard. For large games, it may be effectively impossible — even for computers. Real chess has more positions than atoms in the universe. Perfect backward induction is theoretically correct but practically impossible beyond small games. This is why heuristics, pattern recognition, and machine learning dominate real game-playing.

Behavioral Game Theory — The Fix

Modern game theory incorporates psychology. Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory — people hate losses more than they love equivalent gains — gets embedded into game-theoretic models. Players are modeled as having bounded rationality (limited cognitive capacity), social preferences (caring about fairness and others' outcomes), and time inconsistency (preferences that change depending on when you ask).

The Result

Behavioral game theory is harder, messier, and less elegant than classical game theory — but far more accurate. It's what powers modern behavioral economics, nudge theory, and evidence-based policy design. The field is still developing.
